Dear Minister Burke,

The Commission's mandatory evaluation of Regulation (EU) 2021/821 opened today. This letter consolidates, for the departmental and evaluation record, what has already been established through prior correspondence, committee submissions, and active Ombudsman proceedings. It places before your Department two matters arising from EU regulatory regimes: one is a competent-authority obligation under the dual-use export-control regime, unresolved since 2006; the other concerns the institutional capacity now being established for implementation of the AI Act. Both now sit within the same Department and under the same delegation structure. Both are before the Commission. Both are yours.

## I. THE COMPETENT AUTHORITY OBLIGATION

Section 3 of the Control of Exports Act 2023 designates the Minister for Enterprise, Tourism and Employment as Ireland's competent authority for the purposes of Regulation (EU) 2021/821. That designation rests with you.

By S.I. 241/2025 —the Enterprise, Tourism and Employment (Delegation of Ministerial Functions) (No. 2) Order 2025— the substantive statutory powers under sections 12 and 13 of the Act, including the power to make orders controlling non-Annex-I items and to determine case-specific authorisation requirements, were delegated to Minister of State Niamh Smyth TD. The delegation is legally valid. It does not, by itself, supply the coordination the Regulation requires.

Where a statutory delegation places the exercise of a CA function in a single officer, but the discharge of that function requires interdepartmental inputs the delegatee cannot independently access, the delegating authority must supply the coordination those inputs require. Delegation without the necessary coordination does not complete the administrative architecture required to discharge the function under a Regulation that expressly mandates cooperation between competent authorities. The resulting obligation is to rectify the departmental architecture the delegation presupposes but does not create —and which neither S.I. 241/2025 nor any other instrument currently supplies.

Article 23(2) of Regulation 2021/821 requires direct cooperation between competent authorities, with the stated objectives of efficiency and consistent and effective implementation and enforcement throughout the customs territory of the Union. The corresponding Irish administrative task requires cooperation between three institutions that together hold the constituent functions and information necessary to a CA assessment: DETE, DFA, and the Department of Defence. No statutory mechanism currently compels that cooperation. That absence is a governance gap, not a minor administrative deficiency.

Article 28 requires Member States to equip their competent authorities with the means to gather information and establish whether export-control measures are being properly applied. A formal designation satisfies neither requirement in substance.

The functional question has six components: whether the designated authority can be identified; whether it possesses the information, expertise, and resources to act; whether it can reliably obtain information held elsewhere in the State; whether an institutional mechanism exists requiring cross-departmental inputs to be assembled where the assessment demands them; whether there is an effective route from assessment to regulatory action; and whether responsibility for the resulting determination is clearly attributable where the function is delegated. The first of those components is satisfied on the face of Irish law. The remaining five are open on the evidence of this record.

Two provisions of the Control of Exports Act 2023 bear directly on this record.

Section 12 empowers the Minister to make an order controlling an otherwise non-Annex-I dual-use item where the statutory conditions are met. Section 13 empowers the competent authority to determine, in an individual case, that an authorisation is required for a particular non-Annex-I item. The section 13 mechanism is available. Its conditions are met. The submission is on the departmental record. That determination —applied to the submitted corpus— is what twenty years of engagement has not produced.

Section 29 of the Act requires the Minister to decide export-authorisation applications having regard to Article 15 of Regulation 2021/821, including considerations of national security, public order, EU and international obligations, and human rights. Article 15 and section 29 between them define the considerations a competent authority must weigh. Weighing them requires simultaneous inputs from three institutions: the licensing function (DETE), the regime membership and international coordination function (DFA), and the security assessment function (the Department of Defence). No statutory mechanism currently requires those inputs to be assembled. The current architecture distributes the constituent elements of a decision across three institutions without establishing which body is responsible for producing the determination those elements must yield.

The Department of Enterprise, Tourism and Employment assumed the CA designation on 22 August 2024.

That transfer carried the designation. It did not, on the record available, include any audit, discharge, or determination of the accumulated unresolved record arising during DFA's fifteen years as designated authority.

The DFA produced zero evaluations and zero statutory determinations across that period on a corpus of Operations Research materials submitted from May 2006. No pre-transfer audit was conducted. No mechanism required DETE to assess material held, unprocessed, by its predecessor authority.

The submission predates Regulation 2021/821 itself. The corpus was placed before Irish State authorities in May 2006, under Regulation (EC) No 1334/2000, then Regulation (EC) No 428/2009 from August 2009, then Regulation (EU) 2021/821 from September 2021. The non-determination spans three regulatory instruments and twenty years. It was not resolved at any point of transition. It has compounded with each one.

Your Department has been on notice of the compliance record since at least April 27th, 2026, when the _Interregnum Nullificans_ was transmitted as part of a distribution to the Joint Committee on European Union Affairs and a wide range of ministerial and parliamentary offices. A revised version of that document is attached.

Further correspondence was sent on May 27th, 2026: that letter was addressed to the outgoing COMPET Chair, in which you were named (and copied) both as incoming COMPET Chair and as the minister responsible for the CA designation. This placed the matter before you in that additional capacity. Your Department acknowledged that correspondence under reference DETE-MBURK-01636-2026.

Four prior reference numbers establish the record your Department has inherited:

— DFA-TMO-05256-2024: the DFA's acknowledgement, at the moment of the CA transfer, under the Tánaiste's office.  
— DETE-MBURK-01636-2026: DETE's acknowledgement of the May 27th correspondence.  
— DOD-MO-01201-2025: the ministerial determination issued by the Department of Defence on February 4th, 2026, which purported to close this matter.  
— The Secretary General of DETE, Mr Declan Hughes, has been copied on related correspondence directed to the Joint Committee on Defence and National Security under reference S-2025-0174-DEF, which records the position of both Secretaries General.

On DOD-MO-01201-2025, consider these three points:

First — the legal point. The Department of Defence held no CA designation under Regulation 2021/821 at any material time. A determination it issues on the substance of a dual-use submission has no regulatory standing under the Regulation. It does not constitute a decision in the Regulation's terms. It does not extinguish the obligation held by the designated authority. DOD-MO-01201-2025 does not discharge DETE's obligations.

Second — the source it relied upon. The determination rested entirely on a conclusion issued on June 1st, 2025, by the Office of Emergency Planning. The OEP is a Secretariat function within the Department of Defence. It is not a department, not a statutory body, and holds no independent legal personality. It has never been designated as a competent authority under any version of the dual-use regime. Its advisory role within Defence does not give its conclusions regulatory weight under an instrument it has no standing to apply.

The OEP's engagement with this corpus spans three irreconcilable positions across twenty years. In May 2006 it acknowledged that the petitioner may have information of interest to the State, invited further submission, stalled on a credentials condition, and left the matter without destination or referral. In July 2009 it declined on remit grounds. In June 2025 —five months after Storm Éowyn, during which the OEP was operationally overwhelmed by the precise class of emergency the corpus was designed to address— it issued a substantive adequacy conclusion and concluded that the subject matter was covered by existing implementations. Neither prior position was reconciled. No evaluation of the corpus was conducted. The formal rebuttal of June 3rd, 2025, went unanswered in the eight months before the foreclosure was issued.

When the record was pressed further for a government position on the substantive material, no tangible response was supplied —only the same generic assertions repeated without evidence, supervision, or engagement with the specific submission. This matter is currently before the Office of the Ombudsman, ref. OMB-260331-CSD014.

Three irreconcilable positions across twenty years, none reconciled, do not constitute an assessment. They constitute the record of its absence.

Third — the Article 12 dimension. Article 12 requires the competent authority, when assessing a submission, to take into account the national security interests of the Member State. The security assessment architecture necessary to discharge that consideration resides in the Department of Defence. Neither the DFA across fifteen years nor DETE since August 2024 engaged it. The OEP's unsupervised advisory conclusions do not constitute engagement of that architecture.

The Commission's mandatory evaluation of this Regulation under Article 26 is now open. Ireland's zero-determination record will enter that evaluation as a compliance fact. Your Department has also opened a public consultation inviting stakeholder input into that evaluation, with a deadline of October 15th, 2026; the corpus that is the subject of this notice is the most directly relevant submission that consultation could receive. An Ombudsman complaint (OMB-260331-CSD014) is active. High Court proceedings are in preparation under Order 84 RSC.

## II. THE AI OFFICE AND THE INSTITUTIONAL CAPACITY QUESTION

A convergent institutional question now sits alongside the foregoing within the same Department. The Regulation of Artificial Intelligence Act 2026, signed into law on July 21st 2026, established Oifig IS na hÉireann (the AI Office of Ireland) as an independent statutory body within your Department, with Mr Paul Byrne as Chief Executive Officer. Both functions now sit within the same Department, under the same ministerial delegation structure. This is material, not incidental.

The AI Act (EU Regulation 2024/1689) does not alter the obligations arising under Regulation 2021/821; the two instruments govern distinct regulatory functions. Their relevance here is institutional: both now operate within DETE, with the substantive export-control and AI functions placed under the same ministerial delegation.

Herbert A. Simon —one of the founders of artificial intelligence, Nobel laureate in Economics, polymath, architect of bounded rationality— described the persistent distinction between OR and AI as historically contingent and institutionally artificial. In 1987, he wrote the following:

> **"But it is preferable to stress continuities rather than differences. Instead of differentiating between OR and AI, we need to confuse, blend, and synthesize them as much as possible."**
>
> — _Interfaces 17(4)_, 1987

He then goes further:

> **"We need to build our professional institutions and organizations to use them together, supporting, reinforcing, and extending each other."**
>
> — _Ibid._

The two are sister subjects: a fact not explicitly present in any AI-facing regulation. Ireland has now built for AI precisely the coordinating capacity whose absence is apparent in the export-control record. The technical content of Article 9 of the AI Act is substantially within the established analytical domain of Operations Research: lifecycle risk identification, estimation, evaluation, optimisation of mitigation measures, testing against defined metrics and probabilistic thresholds, and iterative reassessment. The Regulation does not prescribe OR as a methodology; it does, however, prescribe a class of analytical activity in which OR is an established technical discipline — and it does not merely require a policy statement that risks have been considered. The specific analytical tools that process applies to that lifecycle are well-established within OR and its sub-domains: fault-tree analysis, Monte Carlo simulation, extreme-value analysis, reliability modelling and condition-based optimisation.

The point is not that the AI Act prescribes Operations Research as a methodology. It is that Ireland has now created an institutional framework for a regulatory regime whose technical demands substantially overlap with a discipline that the State has possessed in its own custody since 2006.

The risk management lifecycle the AI Act mandates and the OR risk-and-reliability discipline address the same problem. OR sometimes falls under the umbrella of Management Science, but its use in governance and recognition by legislation is lagging.

The State has mishandled a corpus of technically specialised Operations Research material since 2006, while the functions necessary to assess it have remained distributed between departments without an identified mechanism for assembling the requisite expertise, information and security inputs into a competent-authority determination.

In establishing the AI Office, Ireland has expressly recognised that a distributed regulatory model requires coordination, information-sharing and centralised access to technical expertise. The export-control record presents the inverse problem: DETE, DFA and Defence each hold part of the information or institutional function necessary to assess the corpus, yet the record identifies no equivalent mechanism by which those inputs are assembled into a competent-authority determination.

The AI Office is tasked with coordinating relevant competent authorities, facilitating cooperation and information-sharing, and facilitating centralised access to technical expertise where required. Ireland has answered that institutional question affirmatively in the AI context. The export-control record demonstrates that the same institutional question remains unresolved in relation to the older OR corpus.

## III. WHAT IS ASKED

This letter consolidates, for the evaluation record now open, a non-determination spanning twenty years —from May 2006 under Regulation (EC) No 1334/2000, through Regulation (EC) No 428/2009 from August 2009, to Regulation (EU) 2021/821 from September 2021— confirming that the obligation resides with your Department as the designated competent authority under the Control of Exports Act 2023.

The High Court, acting within its ordinary jurisdiction under Order 84 RSC, can compel performance of this obligation by mandamus. The conditions are met. Every available domestic remedy has been pursued. The question is whether the determination comes from the minister or from the court.

I ask that your Department:

(a) confirm receipt of this notice and assign a formal reference number;

(b) confirm whether a statutory determination under Regulation 2021/821 —or a case-specific determination under section 13 of the Control of Exports Act 2023— in respect of the submitted OR corpus has been issued or is in preparation;

(c) identify the mechanism, if any, by which your Department currently assembles the cross-departmental inputs required by Article 12 and section 29 —that is, the licensing function (DETE), the regime membership and international coordination function (DFA), and the security assessment function (the Department of Defence)— in producing an integrated competent authority determination;

(d) identify the mechanism, if any, by which the Department, together with the AI Office, will secure the technical expertise required to support the coordinated implementation and supervision of the EU AI Act where assessment of high-risk systems requires specialised expertise in Operations Research, quantitative risk analysis, optimisation or probabilistic modelling, having regard to the AI Office's stated function of facilitating centralised access to technical expertise for competent authorities;

(e) confirm whether the Department's public consultation on the Commission's evaluation of Regulation 2021/821 constitutes an available channel through which the submitted corpus may now receive the statutory determination it has not received across twenty years.

I ask that you place this correspondence on the formal departmental record and bring it to the attention of Secretary General Mr Declan Hughes and Minister of State Niamh Smyth TD.
