## The Governing Proposition

The Department of Enterprise, Trade and Employment assumed the competent authority
designation under Regulation (EU) 2021/821 on 22 August 2024. That designation carries
a continuing statutory and administrative responsibility: the CA must assess formally
submitted material with dual-use characteristics, within statutory timeframes, and
produce a reasoned determination.

An Operations Research corpus with formally recognised dual-use characteristics was
submitted to Irish authorities in May 2006. It has received no statutory determination
in nineteen years — first under the Department of Foreign Affairs, and now, by
transfer-without-discharge, as an unresolved inherited obligation of DETE.

This administrative failure has persisted through Ireland's 2026 EU Council Presidency,
across which Ireland chairs Council formations with specific competence for dual-use
technology governance and institutional coherence. The Commission's mandatory evaluation
of Regulation (EU) 2021/821 under Article 26 opens on 10 September 2026.

Three parliamentary questions follow from that proposition. They are addressed below
to the committee or body whose mandate most precisely requires each one.

## A Preliminary Note: The Government's Own Framing

The Government's published account of the Regulation of Artificial Intelligence Act
2026 states that the EU AI Act "is a technical implementing measure and does not add
to the obligations placed on regulated entities by the EU Regulation."

That framing requires attention before the parliamentary questions are put, because
it will be the first line of departmental response to any question that invokes the
AI Act in this context.

The framing is accurate in one narrow respect: the AI Act does not add to the
substantive obligations of regulated entities — providers, deployers, importers —
beyond what the underlying EU Regulation already imposes. That is what "technical
implementing measure" means in the context of domestic transposition.

It is inaccurate in the respect that matters here. The AI Act's obligations on
*National Competent Authorities* — the bodies designated to assess, surveil, and
enforce — are not addressed by that framing at all. Arts. 9, 13, 14, 27, and 43 of
Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 impose substantive conformity assessment obligations on
NCAs that did not exist under any predecessor instrument. These are not obligations
on regulated entities. They are obligations on the State — specifically on the AI
Office, which the Government's own account acknowledges is expected to be operational
from 2 August 2026, charged with providing "strategic governance in relation to the
AI Act while facilitating a coordinated and consistent regulatory approach across
Ireland's designated competent authorities."

The "coordinated and consistent regulatory approach across Ireland's designated
competent authorities" is the Government's own formulation for what Article 70 of
the AI Act requires of the single point of contact. It is also an exact description
of what is currently absent in Ireland's dual-use CA architecture, where DETE, DFA,
and the Department of Defence each hold a component of the assessment function with
no statutory coordination mechanism between them. The Government has framed the
AI Office's mandate in terms that the existing DETE/DFA/DoD fragmentation defeats
on its face.

The Digital Omnibus Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026, defers
certain developer obligations (Annex III high-risk systems: 2 December 2027). It
does not defer NCA assessment obligations, Art. 70 coordination requirements, or
the Article 26 evaluation of Regulation (EU) 2021/821. The deferral is not a
defence to the questions below.

## Question 1 — Joint Committee on Enterprise, Tourism and Employment

**Why has DETE not discharged its departmental responsibility under
Regulation (EU) 2021/821?**

DETE assumed the competent authority designation on 22 August 2024. Under Articles
9(2), 12, and 14 of the Regulation, the designated CA is required to maintain and
exercise the capacity to assess formally submitted dual-use material, to make
determinations within statutory timeframes, and to notify those determinations with
reasons.

The OR corpus submitted in May 2006 was not formally disclosed to DETE at the point
of transfer from DFA. The question of whether it was or was not is itself a question
DETE is best placed to answer: a departmental record search, initiated by or under
the Committee's direction, would establish the custodial position within days.

If the record was not disclosed: DFA retains the historical obligation; DETE inherited
the CA designation without institutional memory of the outstanding submission; and
the transfer produced a structural vacancy — a CA with designation but without the
accumulated obligation it was supposed to carry. The remedy is a directed audit of
the DFA–DETE transfer record and a determination that closes the nineteen-year gap.

If the record was disclosed: DETE holds a nineteen-year-old unresolved submission
for which the twelve-month determination clock under Article 12 expired in 2007.
The remedy is the same.

Either way, the Committee's mandate — ministerial accountability for enterprise
legislation and its enforcement — is engaged directly. The question is not whether
DETE knew. The question is whether, now that it does, it will discharge the function
it was designated to hold.

The AI Act dimension sharpens this. The Government's own account positions the AI
Office as the coordination layer across Ireland's designated competent authorities.
DETE is one of those authorities. The AI Office cannot coordinate across a CA
function that is itself fragmented across three departments with no statutory
coordination mechanism. The enterprise committee scrutinises DETE; DETE holds
the CA designation; the AI Office's coordinating mandate depends on that
designation being functional. The chain is short, and the Committee sits at one end
of it.

## Question 2 — Joint Committee on European Union Affairs

**How can Ireland credibly participate in EU implementation and enforcement
of Regulation (EU) 2021/821 while this domestic administrative deficiency persists?**

Ireland chairs the Council of the EU from 1 July 2026. The General Affairs Council,
which Minister of State Byrne chairs in that capacity, holds specific competence for
institutional coherence and Member State compliance with treaty obligations. The
COMPET formation — within which Regulation (EU) 2021/821 sits at the
Council–Parliament interface — is presided over by Irish officials whose Government
has a zero-determination record under that Regulation across the full duration of its
predecessor and recast instruments.

The Commission's Article 26 evaluation opens 10 September 2026. It will examine
whether Ireland's CA architecture constitutes a functioning competent authority. On
the record as it stands — designation transferred without audit, outstanding submission
undisclosed, three-body fragmentation with no coordination mechanism — Ireland will
enter that evaluation as the Presidency state while the Commission assesses whether
its own CA function is operational.

The question this Committee is mandated to examine is the one the JCEUA declined to
examine in October 2025: whether the principle of sincere cooperation under Article
4(3) TEU is being discharged by a Member State that simultaneously holds a Presidency
role with competence for dual-use governance and a domestic CA vacancy in precisely
that governance area.

The AI Act adds a second dimension. Ireland's AI Office is expected to facilitate
a "coordinated and consistent regulatory approach across Ireland's designated
competent authorities." That is an Article 70 function — Ireland's single point of
contact obligation under the AI Act. The AI Office cannot discharge Article 70
consistently while the CA architecture it is meant to coordinate remains fragmented.
A Member State chairing Council discussions on AI governance while its own NCA
coordination architecture is deficient is the same structural contradiction, at
the AI Act level, as the one this Committee has before it at the dual-use level.

The Committee's Standing Order mandate includes examining whether the principle of
subsidiarity is being respected and whether Ireland's EU obligations are being
discharged. Both are engaged here — not as abstract propositions but as specific,
dated, documentable failures visible in the Commission's own evaluation window.

## Question 3 — Joint Committee on Public Petitions and the Ombudsmen / Office of the Ombudsman

**What parliamentary and administrative mechanism exists to examine the underlying
failure after the ordinary executive channels have been exhausted?**

The ordinary executive channels have been exhausted. The record is as follows:

The DFA held the CA designation from 2000 to August 2024. It produced no determination
on the submitted OR corpus across that period. The transfer to DETE occurred without
a formal audit of the accumulated obligation. The Oireachtas committee system, across
seven committees formally addressed between September 2025 and March 2026, produced
five formal declinations, one informal acknowledgement without determination, and
eighteen documented silences in the committee charged specifically with oversight of
the petition mechanism. No committee produced a statutory account of inapplicability.
No committee identified a body to which the matter was competently referred. The SSCEUST
declined on 15 April 2026 (ref. SSCEUST-I-2026-074) on remit grounds without referral.
The Ombudsman complaint (OMB-260331-CSD014) is active.

The question for this Committee and for the Ombudsman is structural, not personal.
No individual officer has been accused of bad faith. The pattern — remit displacement,
acknowledgement-as-closure, deferral without destination, sufficiency assertion,
record suppression, abandonment-without-referral — is the architecture of a system
that has configured itself to avoid the corrective decision. The Qualifying Submissions
(Duty to Determine) Bill 2026, currently in preparation, addresses that architecture
prospectively. The question before this Committee is what mechanism exists now, within
the current institutional configuration, to examine it retrospectively.

The Ombudsman's mandate — investigation of maladministration in public bodies — is
the residual accountability mechanism the ordinary executive channels have not engaged.
The Ombudsman complaint names the DFA as primary respondent. The analysis above adds
DETE's inherited obligation as a second dimension: if the transfer-without-discharge
is itself a maladministration event — a CA designation conveyed without the accumulated
obligation it was supposed to carry — then DETE's position is not that of a body that
failed to act on knowledge it had. It is that of a body that was structurally denied
the knowledge it needed to act. That is a distinct maladministration category, and one
the Ombudsman is specifically empowered to investigate.

The public petitions route cannot substitute for a determination — as the Duty to
Determine Bill's section 13 makes explicit and as the JCPP's own eighteen silences
have demonstrated in practice. What this Committee can do, and what the Ombudsman
can do, is name the structural condition on the record: not as an accusation against
any individual committee or officer, but as the documented output of an architecture
that requires a remedy no single body in the current configuration is empowered to
supply on its own.

## The OR/AI Convergence — Why This Matters Beyond the Administrative Record

The administrative failure documented above has a downstream consequence that extends
beyond the specific submitted corpus. It is worth placing on the record before each
of these committees, because it bears directly on each committee's substantive mandate.

Herbert Simon — Turing Award laureate, Nobel laureate in Economics, and the figure
whose Logic Theorist (1956) is the first identifiable AI program — stated in interviews
with historian Pamela McCorduck (1974–75) that "we were doing AI before [Dartmouth],
we just called it operations research." His 1987 paper "Two Heads Are Better than One:
The Collaboration between AI and OR" formalised the thesis: the two fields share common
roots and address a common problem — how to make good decisions under conditions of
uncertainty and constraint.

The EU AI Act's conformity assessment architecture reproduces that logic without
naming it. The NCA obligations under Arts. 9, 13, 14, 27, and 43 — evaluating
probabilistic risk claims, verifying performance bounds under foreseeable misuse,
assessing human oversight adequacy, structuring Fundamental Rights Impact Assessments
— are, in substance, OR obligations. A competent authority that cannot independently
discharge those obligations will rely on developer documentation: the analytical
capture dynamic documented in Gatekeeping Brief §IV.

Ireland's AI Office was established into a state apparatus that has configured itself,
across nineteen years of non-determination, to avoid engaging OR on its merits. No
statute excluded OR from public administration. The exclusion was procedural: each
sufficiency assertion, each remit declination, each transfer without discharge,
accumulated into a condition in which OR methodology is absent from the Irish public
sector not because it was found unsuitable but because it was never evaluated. That
is foreclosure-by-proxy — and the AI Office inherits the analytical gap it produced.

The Annex III high-risk categories most likely to be deployed in Irish public services
— AI systems evaluating access to essential services (Point 3), AI systems used by
competent authorities to assess risk (Point 6), AI systems for administration of
justice (Point 8) — are also the categories where OR methodology is most directly
applicable and most structurally absent. Irish public bodies will be required to
deploy and oversee such systems from 2 December 2027. They will do so without
sovereign OR assessment capacity, because that capacity was foreclosed before the
AI Act made its absence consequential.

For the Enterprise Committee: this is a value-for-money and enterprise development
failure. OR methodology spans thirty-five primary sub-domains — logistics, healthcare
operations, public resource allocation, infrastructure planning, emergency response.
None requires specialist infrastructure in 2026. The barrier is institutional, not
technical, and it is the direct output of the non-determination documented in this
record.

For the EU Affairs Committee: this is a subsidiarity compliance failure. Ireland's
claim to exercise NCA functions under the AI Act presupposes OR assessment capacity.
The subsidiarity question — whether those functions are being exercised at the level
closest to the citizen with sufficient analytical infrastructure — is one this
Committee is specifically empowered to examine under Standing Order 115(1)(a).

For the Petitions Committee and the Ombudsman: this is the downstream harm the
administrative failure has produced. The specific submitted corpus is one instance.
The structural absence of OR in Irish public administration is the aggregate consequence
of nineteen years of non-determination applied, by institutional momentum, to an
entire discipline. The Ombudsman complaint documents the instance. What exists behind
it is a suppression, by procedural default, of entire classes of public sector
optimisation — in health, housing, transport, and emergency management — that the
Irish state has never decided it did not want.
