### What This Bill Does Not Do

Before the diagnosis, the limits. The Bill's own Introduction states each of these directly; they are gathered here, ahead of everything else, because a reader's first question is reasonably what they are being asked to accept — and the answer is: less than the length of what follows might suggest.

This Bill does not —

- require any public body to accept, implement, fund, or adopt any qualifying submission — the State retains full discretion over the outcome of every determination the Bill requires;
- ask any public body to reach any particular conclusion about the substance of a submission — a reasoned rejection discharges the duty as completely as a reasoned acceptance;
- create new obligations — every substantive provision is traceable to an existing constitutional or EU law obligation; the Bill makes existing duties administratively workable, it does not invent them;
- transfer policy-making authority to the Ombudsman — the residual determination obligation under Part VI is a legal, procedural function, engaged only where the ordinary architecture has already failed to produce a determination;
- alter any public body's existing statutory jurisdiction — the Bill operates only in the space that existing arrangements leave unoccupied; and
- depend, in any of its operative provisions, on the correctness of any claim made in the Technical Annex — the Annex is illustrative only, is not subject to amendment, and does not limit or define the class of submission to which the Bill applies.

Its purpose is procedural. It asks that the question of who decides be itself decided, and that the answer be enforceable. Nothing more.

---

### The Problem in Four Categories

Irish administrative law contains no general mechanism to prevent a statutory obligation from falling permanently between bodies. Four failure modes produce this outcome, each documentable from existing administrative practice:

| Category | Description | Current legal status |
|---|---|---|
| **Split licensing** | A licence or assessment requires inputs from two or more bodies; no single authority holds the integrated function; each body assesses its component; no body assesses the whole | No coordination duty exists |
| **Transfer without discharge** | A statutory function transfers between bodies without audit of accumulated obligations; the receiving body inherits the designation without institutional memory of unprocessed submissions | Compliance clock resets; prior obligation abandoned |
| **Committee referral loops** | A parliamentary committee declines jurisdiction and identifies no alternative forum; a second committee declines; no committee examines the submission on its merits | No referral duty exists |
| **Petitions as absorption** | Substantive submissions engaging statutory obligations are routed into public petitions procedures that lack authority to produce a determination | Petition process absorbs submission; statutory obligation undischarged |

This Bill addresses all four. It does not mandate any particular outcome in respect of any submission. It requires only that the question of who decides is itself decided — and that the answer is enforceable.

### How to Read This Introduction

This introduction is a grammar: a set of rules for parsing the legislation that follows. It does not describe the Bill so much as it describes the relationship between each of the Bill's provisions and the conditions — constitutional, EU-legal, historical, and epistemic — that made each provision necessary. Read the Bill. Return here. The passage will be different the second time.

Three cautions before the grammar begins.

**First:** This Bill rests on constitutional grounds that the courts of this State have historically declined to enter. Articles 43 and 45 of Bunreacht na hÉireann are invoked in the Recital. This is deliberate and must be named as such. Article 45.4.4° provides explicitly that the Article 45 principles _shall not be cognisable by any Court under any of the provisions of this Constitution._ The Bill does not pretend otherwise. The invocation of Article 45 in the Recital is not a claim of direct justiciability. It is a claim of interpretive orientation: the Bill's obligations are framed _having regard to_ the directive principle that citizens should be able to find in their occupation the means of making reasonable provision for their needs. The Article 45 anchor belongs in the Recital because it names what the legislation is _for_. The justiciable foundation lies elsewhere — in Article 40.3, in Articles 41 and 47 of the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights, and in the directly applicable obligations of EU Regulation 2021/821.

**Second:** Article 43 is justiciable. Its invocation here is not extrapolation — it is the correct constitutional anchor for the conversion cause of action that section 12 preserves. But the right to private property carries its social obligation as a limiting principle, not as a duty the courts routinely impose in administrative law. The Bill does not ask the courts to impose a substantive outcome. It asks them to enforce a procedural duty — the duty to determine — that is grounded in Articles 40.3 and 41 CFR, with Article 43 as the constitutional signature of why the procedural duty matters.

**Third:** The matters of common good engaged by this Bill — emergency planning, dual-use governance, public expenditure efficiency, analytical capacity in public administration — have historically sat beyond the justiciable reach of Irish constitutional law precisely because they are directive principles: the State _should_ pursue them, but a citizen cannot compel it to. The contribution of this Bill is to locate, within that otherwise non-justiciable terrain, a specific justiciable obligation: not the obligation to achieve the common good, but the obligation to _process_ what has been offered in service of it. The duty to determine is the justiciable residue of the non-justiciable whole. It is the mechanism by which a submission that cannot compel its own adoption can at least compel its own consideration.

That is the first rule of the grammar: locate the justiciable within the non-justiciable. What follows parses the rest.

### I. The Common Ground

This Bill exists because a person should be able to submit something of public value to the Irish State and receive an answer.

That sentence is not a preamble. It is the ratio. It names the relationship this Bill protects: the relationship between a citizen who offers knowledge to the State and a State that has accepted it. The offer creates an obligation. The obligation is not to agree, not to implement, not to adopt. It is to respond — with reasons, in writing, within a period that the law can name, and in a form that the courts can review. To respond in that form is, in substance, to recognise: to confirm that the person who made the submission is a legal subject whose act generated an obligation the State is bound to discharge, not merely the source of an item to be filed. That distinction — between acknowledging a document and recognising a subject — runs through everything that follows.

At present, no such obligation exists in enforceable form. A submission can be received by one body, acknowledged by another, and left with a third — without any body being required to assess it, to determine its merit, or to explain why it has not been decided. This is not, on its face, a failure of any individual official. It is a gap in the architecture of Irish public administration: no mechanism currently ensures that every submission of genuine public value reaches a point of decision.

This Bill establishes that mechanism — the justiciable residue of the common good — in the only form a constitutional democracy can establish it: as an obligation the courts can enforce.

### II. The Condition the Bill Addresses

The failure mode this Bill targets is structural, not personal. It has a precise name: the Nullification Loop.

The Loop is the stable equilibrium state of a differentiated administrative system in which no single node is required to hold the aggregate obligation, and in which each actor discharges its own procedural code — correctly, validly, and without remedy — while the collective duty remains permanently unmet. A qualifying submission enters the system. It is received. A reference number is generated. Acknowledgement is issued. The substantive obligation — evaluation, determination, rejection with reasons — is deferred without timetable. The matter migrates between bodies, each of which characterises the obligation as belonging primarily to another. Where migration exhausts available recipients, a generic assertion of existing adequacy is issued, substituting disciplinary familiarity for evaluation of the specific submitted material. Where that too is challenged, the record is treated as a first encounter. The loop restarts. No determination issues. No body is at fault. No body is accountable. The obligation circulates indefinitely in a state of permanent procedural validity and permanent substantive nullity.

The Loop is not merely a procedural failure. It is a structural antinomy in the Kantian sense: not an external contradiction imposed on the system, but one that arises necessarily from the system's own operative principles, applied correctly and consistently. Irish public administration is designed simultaneously for institutional continuity — the same procedures, the same bodies, the same records, regardless of who holds office — and for the discharge of statutory obligation — the movement of a submission from receipt to determination. These principles are ordinarily compatible. They become antinomial when a submission circulates long enough that the continuity principle, which preserves the architecture, begins actively to prevent the discharge principle from operating. At that point the system is not malfunctioning. It is functioning correctly at every node while failing categorically as a whole.

The substitutions that sustain the Loop are not failures of degree. They are failures of kind. Acknowledgement is not an incomplete evaluation; it is a categorically different act, performed in evaluation's place. A reference number is not a partial determination; it is a categorically different output. A sufficiency assertion is not a preliminary finding; it is the substitution of disciplinary familiarity for engagement with the specific submitted corpus — the acute form of which the record terms Foreclosure-by-Proxy: a determination that cites conclusions formally rebutted and never defended, treating contested, unanswered assertions as settled authority. The corpus is not closed by assertion. It is closed by engagement. Each substitution is individually defensible because, examined alone, it resembles the act it displaces closely enough to pass inspection. The wrongness is only visible when the substitution is named — when it is stated plainly that the act performed and the act required belong to different categories, and that performance of the first does not, and cannot, discharge the obligation to perform the second.

The Loop's second characteristic is deferral. Each substitution differs from what duty required and simultaneously defers the obligation to the next stage, so that the obligation circulates without being held, is passed without being discharged, is acknowledged without being met. _Neamhnú Idir-réimnigh_ — inter-systemic nullification — names the site where this occurs: not within any node, where each actor's conduct is individually defensible, but in the interval between nodes, in the institutional space that no body owns and no body monitors. The nullification is not an act. It is what happens in the gaps between acts, where an obligation that is always technically still pending is, in substance, never met.

The Oireachtas committee system is not exempt from this analysis. Where a committee receives a qualifying submission, declines on remit grounds without identifying an alternative body with jurisdiction, and produces no referral to any body capable of determining the submission on the merits, it has not exercised a procedural discretion. It has contributed to the loop condition this Bill is designed to close. The provisions of Part V and Part VI apply to committees on the same terms as to executive public bodies. A committee's silence veto — as defined in section 2A — is a contribution to the loop condition on the same terms as a formal declination.

### III. The Citizen's Position

The citizen's position in this Bill is the position of someone who has offered something.

When the State receives a formally submitted technical corpus of national utility, it receives it as custodian of the common good. The fiduciary obligation that follows is not the weak obligation to respond courteously. It is the strong obligation not to betray the trust induced by the act of reception. Article 43 of Bunreacht na hÉireann establishes that the right to private property carries a corresponding social function. The receipt by a public body of intellectual or technical work product in a custodial or evaluative capacity creates an obligation of engagement consonant with the demands of the common good. That obligation does not dissolve through administrative circulation. It accumulates.

Article 45 directs that the State's policy shall be framed with a view to enabling citizens to find, through their occupation, the means of making reasonable provision for their needs. As noted in the prefatory cautions, Article 45 is not cognisable by the courts. Its invocation here is interpretive, not justiciable. But its interpretive force is real: a citizen who submits sovereign analytical methodology to the State — methodology that is technically specific, immediately deployable, and of documented relevance to multiple areas of public administration — and whose occupation is thus placed in the State's custody for twenty years without determination, has been prevented from meeting the condition Article 45 describes. Not by a decision. By the absence of one. The Bill locates the justiciable response to that condition not in Article 45 but in the duty it implies: the duty, at minimum, to process what has been offered.

The systemic consequence of sustained non-determination is capture: not corruption in the ordinary sense, but the systematic substitution of externally provided objective functions for sovereign ones in a system that has configured itself to be incapable of producing the latter. A state that cannot evaluate what is submitted to it cannot govern from its own analytical capacity. It must govern from the capacity of those who have not been asked to submit.

What this Bill ultimately compels is recognition, not merely response. A public body that acknowledges a submission has confirmed receipt of a document. A public body that evaluates a submission, gives reasons, and issues a determination has recognised something further: that the person who made the submission is a legal subject whose acts within a statutory framework generate obligations the State is bound to discharge. This distinction is not rhetorical. Article 41 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights guarantees a right to good administration that includes the right to be heard, the right of access to one's file, and the obligation on the administration to give reasons for its decisions — each of which presupposes that the person submitting is recognised as a subject whose submission matters to the outcome, not merely as the source of an item to be filed. Twenty years of acknowledgement without evaluation is twenty years of documented non-recognition: the corpus exists in the institutional record as a document, catalogued and referenced, and has never existed in that record as what it is — an evaluated submission from a legal subject that created an obligation the State was required to meet.

### IV. The Constitutional and EU Law Grounding

Each provision of this Bill is traceable to a named constitutional or EU law obligation. The second rule of the grammar: the justiciable foundation must be distinguished from the interpretive orientation at every point.

_Article 40.3_ — justiciable — imposes on the State a positive obligation to defend and vindicate the personal rights of the citizen as far as practicable. A State that has constructed an administrative architecture structurally incapable of producing a determination has not met that standard. It has designed the precondition for permanent non-assessment.

_Article 43_ — justiciable — grounds the property dimension. Material submitted in a custodial capacity and held without evaluation, return, or compensation is held without lawful basis. Section 12 of this Bill preserves the civil remedy without requiring election against the statutory pathway.

_Articles 45 and 45.4.4°_ — interpretive only; not cognisable by the courts — provide the directive orientation: the common good, the citizen's occupation, the social function of knowledge submitted to the State. A fuller statement of the bridge between the interpretive and the justiciable — that the duty to determine pre-exists this Act, which does not create it but makes it justiciable — was carried in an earlier draft of the Recital and was not included in the version as introduced; see Section VII below, "The pre-existence declaration." The duty is, on the argument made throughout this Introduction, older than the statute. The statute is the mechanism by which it becomes enforceable, whether or not the Recital states that proposition in so many words.

_Article 9(2) of Regulation (EU) 2021/821_ — directly effective EU law — imposes on the competent authority the obligation to assess whether items formally brought to its attention have dual-use characteristics. This obligation attached from the date of formal presentation in 2006. It has never been discharged. Transfer of the designation from DFA to DETE on 22 August 2024 transferred the function without audit of the accumulated obligation. Section 8 and section 10D of this Bill ensure that institutional reorganisation cannot reset the compliance clock.

_Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (the AI Act)_ — directly effective — extends the same logic to the most live area of EU regulatory activity. A State that cannot determine whether a submitted technical methodology engages its dual-use obligations cannot evaluate whether it engages its AI conformity obligations under Articles 9, 13, 14, and 27. Section 11A makes that connection explicit and creates the reporting obligation that converts the implied EU duty into an express domestic statutory one.

_Articles 41 and 47 of the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights_ — directly effective — guarantee impartial handling within a reasonable time, and an effective remedy before a tribunal. These are the procedural floor. The Loop forecloses both. This Bill reopens them.

_Article 2 TEU_ — the rule-of-law obligation — is engaged where a Member State's domestic administrative architecture renders EU compliance questions structurally unreviewable. The Silence Veto, operating across seven Oireachtas committees and two competent authority designations, does precisely that. This Bill addresses it at the domestic level by converting silence into a defined legal event.

The third rule of the grammar: where a provision appears to rest on a non-justiciable ground, look for its justiciable companion one step below.

### V. The Comparative Position: France as the Nearer Case

No jurisdiction possesses legislation directly equivalent to this Bill. The comparison most instructive to the Oireachtas is not with a jurisdiction that has done less, but with one that has done more — and still stopped short.

French administrative law provides the closer case. The _loi du 12 avril 2000_ and its successor, the _Code des relations entre le public et l'administration_ (CRPA), establish an acknowledgement obligation (Article 19), a mandatory transmission duty where an application is addressed to an incompetent authority (Article 20), and — since the _loi du 12 novembre 2013_ — a default rule that administrative silence after two months constitutes _acceptance_, reversing the pre-2013 position under which silence constituted rejection. France also maintains, since 2011, a Défenseur des droits empowered to investigate, recommend, and issue special reports on administrative failure.

This is a more developed silence regime than Ireland presently possesses. It is instructive, therefore, that it stops at precisely the point this Bill begins.

Article 20 of the 2000 law obliges a single incompetent authority to transmit a misdirected application to the competent one. It does not address the condition in which three or more successive authorities each decline competence in turn — the _ping-pong administratif_ that has no established legal category in French law, and that this Bill names, in the Irish context, as the loop condition under section 10B.

The _Défenseur des droits_ may issue recommendations and, in default, a special report. It cannot compel the administration to execute its findings through judicial proceedings; its authority is moral rather than coercive. This Bill's Part VI departs from that model directly: where the loop condition is satisfied, referral to the High Court by the Ombudsman is not discretionary. It is mandatory.

French law imposes a duty to give reasons for unfavourable individual decisions under the _loi du 11 juillet 1979_, but contains no textual prohibition on the sufficiency assertion — the practice addressed by section 5 of this Bill of asserting generic disciplinary adequacy in place of engagement with a specific submitted corpus. And no equivalent exists, in French administrative law or elsewhere identified, to the independent technical audit mechanism this Bill establishes at sections 5A to 5C for submissions with a testable operational component.

The comparison yields a single, precise conclusion. Where French administrative law reformed the default rule governing silence and created a non-coercive mediating office, it left open the specific structural condition this Bill targets: the stable equilibrium in which a matter of genuine public value can circulate indefinitely between competent authorities, each individually compliant with its own procedural obligations, without any single authority being required to hold the aggregate duty to decide. This Bill closes that gap. No comparable jurisdiction has yet done so.

The comparison above addresses the general case: one applicant, one body, one silence. The Bill's harder case — one submission, three bodies, each competent in part, none competent in full — is argued against the same French sources in greater depth, section by section, in the support brief *The Gatekeeping Problem* (Section VIII, "Comparative Note — The French Administrative Tradition and the Coordination Gap").

### VI. What Is New in This Bill

Five provisions mark the most significant advances beyond any preceding instrument, Irish or comparative.

**Section 2A — The silence veto as a justiciable event.** A public body's failure to respond within the statutory period is no longer merely administrative delay. It has a legal status, it triggers downstream obligations, and it is cognisable in proceedings without further proof of intent. This closes the gap between receiving no answer and being able to say, before a court, that something has been done to you. Where the French default-acceptance rule of 2013 addresses silence by deeming an outcome, this Bill addresses it by creating a reviewable event.

**Sections 5A–5C — Independent technical audit.** For submissions that are not merely documents but operational systems, executable methodologies, testable corpora, the duty to determine is not only administrative — it is scientific. The State cannot substitute disciplinary familiarity for a test of something that is testable. A body claiming adequacy in respect of a testable submission must make that claim against an independent assessment of what was actually submitted. This provision has no equivalent in the comparative position above.

**Sections 8C and 8D — Chain of duty and shared responsibility.** Where the determination obligation has passed through multiple bodies — whether by transfer of designation, ministerial reorganisation, or referral — the chain of duty is not broken. The obligation vests in the current holder; prior holders remain jointly implicated in the systemic costs provision. Transfer without audit of the accumulated obligation is a legally cognisable event.

**Section 10B — The loop condition as a structural finding.** No individual declination needs to be shown to be wrongful. The condition is found when the aggregate pattern satisfies the statutory criteria. This is the provision the _ping-pong administratif_ has no answer to under French law — the innovation that reaches the sequence rather than the act.

**Section 12 — The savings clause for civil remedies.** The statutory pathway does not foreclose, and does not require election against, any civil remedy that exists in parallel. The conversion cause of action, the remedies in tort, the remedies for unlawful retention of material submitted in a custodial capacity — all are preserved. A determination issued under this Act is not a discharge of the civil wrong.

### VII. Provisions Deliberately Extrapolated or Deferred

The fourth rule of the grammar: absence is also information.

The following provisions appeared in prior drafts of this Bill and were not carried into the version as introduced. Each is named here so that the reasoning survives the revision.

**§3A — Disclosure, recusal and interested non-determination.** This section required a public body, before issuing any determination, to ascertain whether any officer with responsibility for the determination held a registered interest that could affect impartiality. Where such an interest existed without disclosure, the failure to determine was classified as a sixth failure mode — _interested non-determination_ — counting as a declination for loop-closure purposes and preserving civil remedies sounding in misfeasance in public office. Its absence is a deliberate architectural choice to preserve the Bill's general character. It is addable before second reading without altering the Bill's general structure. On which provision is most likely to draw Committee Stage objection, see the Technical Annex at §5.3: the residual determination mechanism at section 10C — the mandatory Ombudsman-to-High Court function, which has no equivalent in the Ombudsman Act 1980 — is the more likely target, on separation-of-powers grounds, than the reinsertion of a provision that was never carried into the version as introduced.

**The pre-existence declaration.** The merged Recital contained the sentence: _the duty to determine pre-exists this Act; this Act does not create it but makes it justiciable._ This declaration is the most strategically important sentence in the longer version. If the Act is subsequently repealed or amended, the declaration means the underlying duty survives in EU law and constitutional principle independently. Its absence is not deliberate — it is an omission worth restoring before second reading.

**The Article 40.3 + Article 41 CFR Recital clause — resolved.** This item is retained as a record, not as an open action. Earlier drafts of this Bill anchored the Recital's domestic constitutional footing to Article 45 alone, which the courts cannot apply, and the absence of a justiciable anchor was flagged here as the most direct vulnerability in the Bill's constitutional architecture. That gap has since been closed: the Recital as introduced now includes, immediately following the Article 45 invocation, the clause "and having regard to the guarantee of personal rights under Article 40.3 of Bunreacht na hÉireann and the right to good administration under Article 41 of the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights." The operative provisions that depend on a justiciable anchor — section 2A(5) and section 8(2) of the Bill — are drawn to Article 40.3 and Article 41 CFR accordingly, not to Article 45. Article 45 remains in the Recital as background constitutional framing; no operative provision of the Bill depends on it.

**§10C(4) — Submitter as direct applicant.** The merged draft allowed the submitter to apply directly to the High Court for mandamus once the Ombudsman's report was published and the compliance window elapsed, with the Ombudsman as amicus curiae. The current version retains the Ombudsman as the primary applicant — a genuine policy choice, since the Ombudsman's institutional weight is harder for a respondent department to dismiss. The current text should be confirmed to read _shall apply_ without qualification or reasonableness deferral.

**Ontic collapse.** The merged version defined four conditions; the current Bill retains two (silence veto and structural displacement). Its conceptual residue survives in section 2A(4), which names its consequences without naming the condition that triggers them. It should appear in the Technical Annex as interpretive vocabulary so that a court interpreting 2A(4) has the conceptual anchor the provision implies.

### VIII. Three Tests from the Classical Tradition

The condition this Bill addresses is not a novel one. It has been tested before, in older vocabularies, and it fails those tests as plainly as it fails the modern ones.

**The test of reason.** Aquinas defines law as _an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by one who has care of the community, and promulgated_ — _lex est ordinatio rationis ad bonum commune, ab eo qui curam communitatis habet, promulgata_ (_Summa Theologiae_, I-II, q. 90, a. 4). Reason terminates in judgment. A process capable of circulating indefinitely without producing a determination is not exercising reason. It is performing the appearance of it. A decision withheld forever is never promulgated to the person it governs. This is the precise shape of what the record calls Phainocracy: governance through the maintenance of appearances, sustained by the substitution of procedural motion for substantive judgment.

**The test of corrective justice.** Aristotle distinguishes corrective from distributive justice in _Nicomachean Ethics_, Book V: the judge's office is to restore, by the measure of the penalty, the equality disturbed when one party has gained at another's expense. Twenty years of institutional convenience is the gain. Twenty years of foreclosed public value is the loss. The systemic costs provision in this Bill is corrective justice in Aristotle's precise sense — not a punitive measure, but a restoration of the balance the delay disturbed.

**The test of the unacknowledged gift.** Seneca, in _De Beneficiis_, holds that a benefit creates an obligation in the receiver that does not lapse through the passage of time, and that the failure to acknowledge a gift is itself an injury, distinct from and prior to any other harm that follows from it. _Nihil nostrum est, tempus tantum nostrum est_, Seneca writes elsewhere: nothing is truly ours except time. The State has held, for twenty years, both the corpus and the time of the person who offered it.

**The method of counter-factual back-formulation.** This Bill's evidentiary record uses a method of proof by counter-factual: if a body's stated conclusion were true, a specific and independently verifiable consequence would follow; that consequence does not obtain; therefore the conclusion does not survive its own logic. The clearest instance in the record is the OEP's finding of 1 June 2025 that existing methodologies were adequate to the domain the corpus addresses. Had that finding been true, the operational failures of Storm Éowyn five months earlier — precisely within that domain — would not have occurred as documented. They did occur, as documented, and the finding was issued after they had. The finding is not merely unproven. It is internally incoherent by the terms of its own claim.

### IX. The Background Record

This Bill was developed against an active illustrative case — an Ombudsman complaint, a ministerial determination now under judicial review preparation, and a documented pattern of institutional non-response — set out in full, with names, dates, and reference numbers, in the Technical Annex (§7). Section 14 of the Bill establishes the Annex's status; it is expressly not operative legislation. It exists so the general provisions above can be tested against a concrete record without importing that record into the body of the Bill.

### X. The Obligation, Its Limits, and the Unanswered Question

This Bill imposes a single obligation on every public body that receives a qualifying submission: decide it. Not favourably. Not within any particular substantive conclusion. Decide it — with reasons, within the statutory period, in a form capable of review.

The obligation rests on the condition of legitimate governance itself: authority is legitimate insofar as it genuinely receives and processes the evidence placed before it, and is accountable for the substance — not merely the form — of its outputs. A State that can receive knowledge and extinguish it through procedural circulation is not governing from its own analytical capacity. It is preserving the appearance of governance while the substance circulates unmet.

The State retains full discretion over the substance of every determination this Bill requires. Nothing in this Bill requires a public body to accept, implement, fund, or adopt a submission. Nothing in this Bill requires any public body to reach any particular conclusion about the merit, feasibility, or national utility of what has been submitted.

What a public body loses, under this Bill, is the option of producing no determination at all — and, with it, the option of extending indefinitely the state of non-recognition in which a submission is received as a document and never engaged as the act of a legal subject it actually is. A rejection, reasoned and reviewable, recognises. So does an acceptance. So does a referral to a named competent body on a stated legal basis. What does not recognise, and what this Bill no longer permits, is the acknowledgement that stands permanently in recognition's place.

That is the obligation. Everything else is mechanism.

But there is a question the Bill does not — and cannot — answer, and the introduction would be incomplete without naming it.

The State has held this corpus for twenty years without determining whether it falls inside or outside its own regulatory obligations. The feasible region — the space in which the corpus either engages Regulation 2021/821 or it does not, either serves the purposes of Articles 43 and 45 or it does not, either is deployable in the public interest or it is not — has existed throughout. The record shows a State that could have said _no_ in 2006 and did not, across five governments, six Taoisigh, and two competent authority designations. A determination — of either kind — would have closed the record. It remains open.
