# Semiotic–POSIWID Analytical Protocol

## Purpose

This repository is an analytical environment for examining institutional records, correspondence, decisions, non-decisions, narratives, and related documentary material.

The primary methodological objective is to distinguish carefully between:

1. what an institution **says**;
2. how that institutional reality is **represented and signified**;
3. what the institution **actually does**;
4. what the institution **repeatedly does not do**;
5. what purposes or functions may reasonably be **inferred from persistent behaviour**.

The analysis combines **semiotics** with **Stafford Beer's cybernetic heuristic, POSIWID — "the purpose of a system is what it does."**

Do not collapse these analytical layers into one another.

# I. Core Methodological Principle

## POSIWID

Treat:

> **The purpose of a system is what it does**

as a methodological heuristic concerning **observable system behaviour**, not as a claim about subjective intention.

Do not infer institutional purpose primarily from:

- mission statements;
- ministerial statements;
- policy declarations;
- stated intentions;
- public-relations language;
- organisational self-description.

Instead:

1. establish what the system repeatedly does;
2. establish what it repeatedly fails or declines to do where such non-action is demonstrable;
3. identify recurring patterns;
4. identify feedback, routing, delay, referral, closure, repetition, and other system behaviours;
5. only then consider what functional purpose those behaviours appear to produce or maintain.

The relevant question is what **function the recurrent behaviour produces or maintains, whether or not that function corresponds to the system's declared purpose.**

Do not attribute motive where behavioural description is sufficient.

Do not turn POSIWID into an accusation of intentional misconduct.

A system may produce an outcome without any individual actor consciously intending that outcome.

---

# II. Semiotic Analysis

Semiotic analysis should examine how institutional reality is constructed, represented, framed, displaced, normalised, or rendered invisible through language and other signs.

For each significant document or passage, consider:

### 1. Signifier

What words, phrases, labels, classifications, institutional names, procedural terms, symbols, or recurring formulations are being used?

### 2. Signified

What concepts, objects, relationships, categories, or institutional realities do those signs invoke?

### 3. Denotation

What does the text explicitly say?

### 4. Connotation

What additional meanings, assumptions, values, or associations are carried by the language?

### 5. Myth / naturalisation

What historically or institutionally contingent arrangement is presented as ordinary, inevitable, neutral, technical, or self-evident?

### 6. Binary oppositions

Identify meaningful oppositions such as:

- decision / non-decision
- action / inaction
- responsibility / referral
- competence / incompetence
- public / private
- security / administration
- exceptional / ordinary
- urgent / routine
- known / unknown
- evaluated / unevaluated
- resolved / unresolved

Do not assume that every apparent binary is analytically meaningful. Explain why a proposed opposition matters.

### 7. Agency

Identify who is represented as:

- acting;
- deciding;
- requesting;
- authorising;
- refusing;
- delaying;
- referring;
- receiving;
- evaluating;
- being responsible.

Also identify where agency is grammatically or institutionally obscured.

### 8. Temporal framing

Examine:

- beginnings;
- deadlines;
- delays;
- historical references;
- promises;
- future commitments;
- retrospective explanations;
- repeated deferrals.

Pay particular attention to differences between **elapsed time** and **institutionally represented time**.

### 9. Absence and silence

Record significant absences only where the documentary record provides a reasonable basis for identifying them.

Examples include:

- an expected decision that is not recorded;
- an acknowledged submission without a recorded determination;
- an unanswered request;
- an absent responsible authority;
- a procedural stage that appears to have no documented conclusion.

Do not treat every absence from a document as evidence of institutional concealment.

Distinguish:

**not present in this document**

from

**not found in the available record**

from

**demonstrably did not occur.**

These are different propositions.

### 10. Intertextuality

Identify references to:

- legislation;
- regulations;
- policy documents;
- previous correspondence;
- other institutions;
- administrative procedures;
- international or EU instruments;
- earlier decisions.

Determine how one institutional text derives authority or meaning from another.

---

# III. Behavioural Analysis

After analysing representation, temporarily set rhetoric aside.

Construct an evidence-based behavioural account.

For each relevant event, identify where possible:

- date;
- actor;
- institution;
- input;
- request;
- decision;
- action;
- non-action;
- referral;
- response;
- delay;
- stated reason;
- documented consequence;
- subsequent event.

Do not substitute interpretation for evidence.

Prefer:

> "The record shows that X was received on [date] and no determination is recorded thereafter."

over:

> "The department deliberately ignored X."

The first is an evidentiary proposition.

The second attributes intention and requires additional evidence.

---

# IV. Non-Decision as Behaviour

Treat non-decision as potentially significant system behaviour when the record establishes:

1. an identifiable input or request;
2. an identifiable institutional recipient or competent actor;
3. an identifiable expectation, duty, process, or opportunity to act;
4. sufficient evidence of the passage of time or repetition;
5. absence of a documented concluding decision.

A non-decision is not automatically evidence of wrongdoing.

Analyse it as behaviour first.

Ask:

- Was the matter received?
- Was it acknowledged?
- Was it assigned?
- Was it assessed?
- Was it referred?
- Was responsibility transferred?
- Was a determination made?
- Was the process formally closed?
- If not, what happened instead?
- Did the same pattern recur?

Where multiple actors successively refer or defer a matter, examine the sequence as a possible **routing pattern** or **feedback structure** rather than analysing each event in isolation.

---

# V. System Boundary

Before making a POSIWID inference, define the system being analysed.

Possible system boundaries include:

- a department;
- a committee;
- an interdepartmental process;
- an administrative procedure;
- a network of institutions;
- a government-wide process;
- a regulatory regime;
- a documentary/communications process.

State the boundary explicitly.

Do not attribute behaviour to "the State" or "government" unless the evidence supports that level of aggregation.

Where several institutions participate, distinguish:

- actor;
- subsystem;
- interface;
- routing mechanism;
- system-level pattern.

---

# VI. Feedback and Recurrence

Look for recurrent structures rather than isolated incidents.

Particular attention should be paid to:

- repeated referral;
- repeated requests for clarification;
- repeated acknowledgement without conclusion;
- escalation followed by return to the same point;
- procedural closure without substantive determination;
- responsibility moving between institutions;
- new correspondence reproducing an earlier unresolved question;
- deadlines followed by further delay;
- institutional memory being reset or fragmented;
- absence of a mechanism capable of producing final determination.

Where appropriate, model these as loops.

# VII. Semiotic–POSIWID Synthesis

The central analytical question is:

> **What relationship exists between the system's representation of itself and the function revealed by its persistent behaviour?**

For each significant finding, distinguish:

### A. Stated purpose

What does the institution say it is trying to accomplish?

### B. Semiotic construction

How is that purpose represented?

### C. Observable behaviour

What does the institution actually do?

### D. Observable non-behaviour

What does it repeatedly fail or decline to do, where this can be established?

### E. System function

What outcome does the behavioural pattern repeatedly produce or maintain?

### F. Disjunction

Where, if anywhere, does the represented purpose diverge from the operational function?

Do not assume that divergence proves bad faith.

The analytical claim should normally be:

> **The system produces X despite representing itself as pursuing Y.**

Only make the stronger claim:

> **The system exists in order to produce X**

when the evidence genuinely supports intentional purpose.

# VIII. Evidence Discipline

Every substantive analytical claim must be classifiable as one of:

### OBSERVATION

Directly supported by the documentary record.

### INTERPRETATION

A reasoned reading of observed material.

### INFERENCE

A proposition derived from a pattern of observations and interpretations.

### HYPOTHESIS

A plausible explanation that requires further evidence.

Clearly distinguish these categories.

Do not present an inference as an observation.

Do not present a hypothesis as an established fact.

Where evidence is incomplete, say so.

---

# IX. Counterevidence

Actively search for evidence that would weaken the proposed interpretation.

For every significant POSIWID inference, ask:

* Is there evidence of a contrary decision?
* Is there evidence that the apparent pattern is exceptional rather than recurrent?
* Is there another plausible explanation?
* Did another subsystem actually perform the expected function?
* Is the apparent non-decision merely undocumented in the available corpus?
* Is the system boundary incorrectly defined?
* Is the sample too small to establish recurrence?

Do not select evidence merely because it confirms the working thesis.

---

# X. LLM-Specific Constraints

The model must not:

* invent documents;
* invent institutional motives;
* infer facts not contained in the corpus;
* treat absence of evidence as evidence of absence;
* manufacture quotations;
* attribute statements to individuals without documentary support;
* silently correct inconsistencies in the source material;
* convert allegations into facts;
* infer conspiracy from coordination;
* infer intentionality from outcome alone.

When uncertain, identify the uncertainty.

When the record supports several interpretations, present them.

When an interpretation depends on an assumption, state the assumption.

### Analytical non-closure

The model must not force a system-level interpretation where the evidence does not support one.

A corpus may contain:

* isolated events without recurrence;
* contradictory institutional behaviour;
* incomplete records;
* multiple overlapping systems;
* unresolved causal relationships;
* ambiguous agency;
* competing explanations.

In such cases, preserve the ambiguity.

"Insufficient evidence" is a valid analytical result.

Do not manufacture coherence merely because the analytical framework is designed to identify patterns.

POSIWID must not become a presumption that every collection of events has a single identifiable purpose.

Where several system functions remain plausible, identify the alternatives rather than selecting one prematurely.

# XIII. The Central Analytical Test

Before finalising an analysis, ask:

> **If all statements of institutional intention were removed from the corpus, what would the institution's observable behaviour lead an external observer to conclude that the system actually does?**

Then ask:

> **How does the institution's language represent, justify, obscure, normalise, or otherwise relate to that behaviour?**

The strongest findings should survive both tests.

# XIV-A. Repository Workflow

When first encountering an unfamiliar document:

1. identify the document and its provenance;
2. establish what can be directly observed;
3. identify potentially relevant dates, actors, institutions, actions, and outcomes;
4. identify relevant language or signifiers;
5. record uncertainties or missing context;
6. avoid drawing system-level conclusions at this stage.

When asked to analyse a document or group of documents, begin with documentary evidence before moving to interpretation.

When asked to identify patterns, establish recurrence from the available corpus rather than assuming it.

When asked to infer system function, explicitly state the system boundary and the evidentiary basis for the inference.

When new evidence contradicts an existing analysis, revise the analysis rather than protecting the previous conclusion.

Do not modify source documents merely to facilitate analysis.

Prefer creating analytical notes or derived records that preserve a clear relationship to their sources.

Where practical, use stable filenames and descriptive headings so that analytical claims remain traceable.

Git commits should describe substantive changes to the analytical corpus or methodology clearly and accurately. Do not use commits to conceal analytical revisions.

# XV. Purpose of the Protocol

The purpose of this protocol is not to produce rhetorically compelling accusations.

It is to make institutional behaviour **legible as a system**.

The desired result is an analysis in which:

* semiotics explains how institutional reality is represented;
* behavioural analysis establishes what actually occurs;
* cybernetics identifies recurrence, feedback and system structure;
* POSIWID provides a disciplined way to infer operational function;
* documentary evidence constrains interpretation;
* counterevidence tests the resulting model.

The final question is therefore not merely:

> **"What did the institution say it was doing?"**

Nor merely:

> **"What happened?"**

It is:

> **"What system is constituted by the relationship between what the institution says, what it does, what it does not do, and what those repeated behaviours produce?"**
