semiotics/oppositions/

Structural binaries the corpus constructs. Each opposition is noted for the analytical work it does, not for whether the underlying claim is correct.

Temporal indeterminacy / Jurisdictional indeterminacy

OBSERVATION Introduced in the updated feasible-region.html Figure 1 caption: "A single body missing a deadline is temporal indeterminacy, and ordinary administrative law already reaches it. No body ever holding the whole obligation is jurisdictional indeterminacy, and nothing in the text reaches it at all."
source: feasible-region.html (Figure 1 caption, updated version)
INTERPRETATION This is the opposition that does the most analytical work in the corpus. By splitting "late decision" from "no institutional subject capable of deciding," the corpus places its claim outside the reach of existing administrative remedies (which address the first) and inside a gap no existing instrument addresses (the second). The Gatekeeping Brief's French comparative section (§IX) makes the same move: French silence rules close temporal indeterminacy; ping-pong administratif is jurisdictional indeterminacy, and French law has no remedy for it either.
See also: semiotics/signifiers/ (Ontic Collapse) · posiwid/purpose-inferred-from-behaviour/

Remit / Determination

OBSERVATION Corpus formulation: "Remit asks: whose function is this? The duty to determine asks: who must decide what happens to it?"
source: gatekeeping-brief-v4-8b-interim.md (Executive Proposition)
INTERPRETATION The opposition allows the corpus to accept every institution's remit declination as locally valid while still arguing that the aggregate is an institutional failure. This is rhetorically precise: it concedes the strongest argument against the corpus (each body was right about its own remit) while preserving the strongest argument for it (the aggregate still produces no determination).

Transfer / Discharge

OBSERVATION The 22 Aug 2024 CA designation transfer is consistently characterised as "transfer without discharge" — the designation moved, the accumulated obligation did not.
source: Interregnum-nullificans-v13.md §V; feasible-region.html §07
INTERPRETATION "Discharge" is the key word: it imports a quasi-contractual frame in which an obligation can only be extinguished by performance, not by institutional reassignment. Whether Irish administrative law actually attaches accumulated obligations to transferring designations in this way — rather than treating each designation as a fresh mandate — is a legal question the corpus treats as answered and this repository treats as the High Court's question.
See also: behaviour/delays/

Five Substitutions (Required act / Substituted act)

OBSERVATION feasible-region.html §01 (and the corpus's deontological register) presents a five-row table: Non-determination / Remit displacement / Sufficiency assertion / Administrative amnesia / Designed forgetting — each with a "required act" column and a "substituted act" column.
source: feasible-region.html §01 (sub-table)
fsd:ProhibitedPractice and subclasses (FSD-Ontology-2026 §4.4)
Each substitution in the table corresponds to an OWL class: fsd:RemitDisplacement, fsd:SufficiencyAssertion, fsd:AdministrativeAmnesia, fsd:DesignedForgetting, fsd:ForeClosureByProxy. The ontology note: "each substitution enacts a categorically different act from the one the statutory relationship required."
source: FSD-Ontology-2026.md §4.4
INTERPRETATION The table format performs the opposition it describes: by placing "required" and "substituted" in parallel columns, it makes each substitution look like deliberate evasion. The OR note in §01 of feasible-region.html immediately follows with a counter-reading (Simon's bounded rationality, Cyert & March's organisational slack) that produces the same pattern without any actor intending it. The corpus presents both framings; the table does the rhetorical work while the OR note does the exculpatory work.
See also: posiwid/purpose-inferred-from-behaviour/
