Explanatory frames the corpus imports from outside the administrative record — philosophy, economics, OR theory, cognitive science. Each is noted for what it adds to the argument, not for whether the analogy holds.

Constraint-satisfaction / Feasible region (OR frame)

OBSERVATION feasible-region.html reads Ireland's constitutional architecture as a constraint-satisfaction problem: each branch holds a formal allocation (the design capacity, shown as dashed outline in Figure 1); actual exercised capacity is the solid fill; the Nullification Loop operates in the gap between them; the EU exogenous constraint progressively shrinks the domestic feasible region.
source: feasible-region.html (title, Figure 1, §09)
INTERPRETATION The frame is doing double work. As an analytical tool it maps formal authority against exercised capacity across branches, which is a legitimate political-science move. As a rhetorical frame it positions the corpus author as an OR practitioner applying the same tools the State should have applied to the OR submission — a neat mise en abyme that makes every failed evaluation look like further evidence of the problem.

Kojève / Hegel recognition dyad (philosophical frame)

OBSERVATION feasible-region.html Figure 2 maps "THE STATE" and "SUBMITTER" onto Kojève's reading of the Hegel master/slave dyad. The loop is characterised as self-sealing because "the recognition the submitter seeks cannot be given by the body that has already refused to recognise what is being submitted." The only exit is a third party (Ombudsman / Court) outside the dyad.
source: feasible-region.html (Figure 2)
INTERPRETATION The Bateson double-bind notation at the base of Figure 2 ("name the minister to ask who the minister is") adds a second philosophical layer. Both move the argument from the administrative to the structural: the system cannot correct itself from within because self-correction requires recognition the system has already denied. This frame is unfalsifiable in its own terms — any failure to respond confirms the loop rather than challenging it. That is not a logical flaw in the frame; it is a feature of structural readings. But it is worth naming as such.

Hart incomplete contracts / residual right (economics frame)

OBSERVATION feasible-region.html §09 synthesis (updated version): "Oliver Hart's account of incomplete contracts holds that no set of governing rules can specify an outcome for every contingency — the design question is not how to write a complete rulebook, but who holds the residual right to decide once the rulebook runs out. 'No node holds the obligation to recognise that the collective function has not been performed' is that same gap, restated for a constitution rather than a firm."
source: feasible-region.html §09 (Hart reading, updated version)
INTERPRETATION Hart's incomplete contracts framework is normally applied to firm governance (who controls the firm when the contract is silent?). Transposing it to constitutional architecture is a move with intellectual precedent (Tirole, Aghion & Bolton on authority structures in organisations) but not in Irish or EU constitutional law. Its value here is prescriptive: it shifts the question from "who failed" to "who should hold the residual right" — and in doing so, generates the legislative remedy (the Qualifying Submissions Bill's mandatory Ombudsman application under s.10C(4)). The narrative justifies the legislative fix, not the other way around.
See also: semiotics/signifiers/ (Ontic Collapse) · posiwid/purpose-inferred-from-behaviour/

Simon / Cyert & March — bounded rationality and organisational slack (cognitive frame)

OBSERVATION feasible-region.html §01 OR note (updated version): "Simon's account of bounded rationality holds that organisations do not optimise, they satisfice — search stops once an answer feels adequate, not once the specific evidence has been examined. The sufficiency assertion is that stopping point, formalised. Cyert and March supply the mechanism underneath it: search only begins when a problem is felt as a problem, and stops once organisational slack absorbs the discomfort of leaving it open. A submission no single node is under pressure to resolve is not a submission anyone decided to bury. It is one that never generated enough friction to be searched for at all."
source: feasible-region.html §01 (updated OR note)
INTERPRETATION This is the corpus's most important internal tension — and its most sophisticated move. The five-substitutions table implies deliberate evasion; the Simon/Cyert & March reading provides a structural explanation that requires no deliberate evasion at all. The corpus presents both, which is analytically honest. The practical consequence of the cognitive frame is that it removes the need to prove intent — and therefore makes the legal argument stronger, not weaker: the system malfunctions structurally regardless of anyone's purpose.
See also: semiotics/oppositions/ (Five Substitutions) · posiwid/purpose-inferred-from-behaviour/

"One field, two names" (Simon AI / OR history)

OBSERVATION The Simon quote ("we were doing AI before Dartmouth, we just called it operations research") appears in three corpus documents as the anchor for the AI Office / OR-suppression argument.
source: ai-office-parallel-argument-v2.md; gatekeeping-brief-v4-8b-interim.md §III; DETE-letter-Sep-10_clean.md §II
INTERPRETATION The quote does not establish that the submitted corpus is AI. It establishes that the disciplinary distinction between OR and AI is historically contingent — which is a legitimate historiographical claim. The argumentative move is to use that historical contingency to argue that the State's recognition of AI governance (AI Office) while failing to engage OR is logically inconsistent. Whether that inconsistency creates a legal obligation is a separate question.
